How I Built My Portfolio (And Why I Kept It Simple)

personal field note · by Vinayak Mishra

Here's the actual thought process and steps behind building my portfolio site from scratch — and why simplicity ended up being the whole strategy for getting noticed as a builder.

The main idea behind the whole build was: fewer decisions for the visitor, not more features for me to show off. A portfolio's job isn't to prove you can use every tool — it's to make one thing obvious fast: who you are and what you build.

That mattered a lot once I started thinking about this not just as a portfolio, but as a way to build a presence — as someone creating in public, not just applying for things quietly.

Why I Built It Myself Instead Of Using A Builder

I could've used Wix or Framer and had something live in an hour. I didn't, on purpose. Writing my own HTML/CSS meant every pixel was a decision I made, not a template someone else designed for a thousand other people. It's slower, but it's mine — and for a builder's portfolio, that difference is the entire point.

  • one index.html, one style.css, one main.js to start
  • no component library, no build step, no dependencies
  • anything new got added only when the site actually needed it

Design Simplification: One Identity, Not A Template

Most portfolio templates try to look "professional" by looking like every other portfolio. I went the other way — dark background, one serif display font for my name, one accent color, a video background instead of a static image, and a single scroll instead of ten pages. It reads as a person, not a resume PDF wearing a website costume.

  • one dominant font pairing — serif for identity, sans for body text
  • one accent color used sparingly so it actually means something
  • motion (video bg, hover states) used to add life, not decoration
  • real personality in the copy instead of generic "passionate developer" language

Content Simplification: Show, Don't List

Early drafts had a skills section, a timeline, a proper "About Me" essay. All of it got cut. What replaced it was shorter and more honest: what I'm currently obsessed with, what I've actually shipped, and what I'm bad at explaining unless someone asks. People remember specific, weird detail — not bullet-pointed skill lists.

Engagement Simplification: Be Findable, Not Loud

The part that actually drives engagement wasn't a feature — it was consistency. Every real project got linked directly instead of described secondhand. The nav always points to the same three things: home, blog, projects. Social links sit in the footer, not scattered across pop-ups. When someone finds the site through GitHub, LinkedIn, or a random DM, it looks and feels exactly the same everywhere.

  • every platform (GitHub, LinkedIn, X, Instagram) links back to the same site
  • the blog exists so there's always a reason to come back
  • projects link straight to working repos, not just screenshots
  • the site updates often enough that it doesn't look abandoned

The Real Standard For A Builder's Site

A personal site doesn't need to do everything a company site does. It needs to answer one question fast — "does this person actually build things?" — and then get out of the way.

  • one clear identity instead of ten competing sections
  • real projects linked directly instead of summarized
  • a blog that shows how you think, not just what you finished
  • consistency across platforms instead of a different persona on each one

That's the whole approach, really. Not a growth hack, not a template — just removing everything that wasn't the actual point, and being consistent enough that people recognize the site the second time they see it.
